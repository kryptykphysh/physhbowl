# Statement

[[Sampson Kempthorne]] was an architect working in and around London. In 1841, he wrote a letter to [[Jonah Magnus]], who was working on esoteric cases in Edinburgh Townhouse at the time, to explain the strange events surrounding [[George Gilbert Scott]]. [[Sampson Kempthorne|Sampson]] and his wife, [[Marianne Kempthorne|Marianne]], were about to leave for New Zealand, which gave him the confidence to speak out.

[[George Gilbert Scott|George]] trained under [[Henry Roberts]], who was in turn trained by [[Robert Smirke]], although [[Robert Smirke|Smirke]] had also tutored [[George Gilbert Scott|George]] personally. He and [[Sampson Kempthorne|Sampson]] collaborated together on a design for a workhouse. However, [[George Gilbert Scott|George]]'s planning methods were strange: he would stare at the plans for hours, before taking them into his office and locking the door. In there, he would mutter and scream to himself, before emerging glistening with effort, new designs in hand. These designs were worse in [[Sampson Kempthorne|Sampson's]] eyes: they sacrificed practicality for symmetry and the spaces became smaller with each iteration.

When [[George Gilbert Scott|George]]'s father died in 1834, he and [[Sampson Kempthorne|Sampson]] parted ways, a relief to [[Sampson Kempthorne|Sampson]]. Shortly afterwards, [[Sampson Kempthorne|Sampson]] was invited to a social function hosted by [[Henry Roberts|Henry]]. Here, he met [[Robert Smirke]], and asked him about [[George Gilbert Scott|George]]'s training. [[Robert Smirke|Robert]] said that he didn't think [[Sampson Kempthorne|Sampson]] realised how much of a lucky escape he had, and that his training had been about balance and equilibrium, a subject about which [[Robert Smirke|Robert]] spoke passionately as "the true goal of the architect". [[Robert Smirke|Robert]] then started to rant, saying that [[George Gilbert Scott|George]] took too many shortcuts and that he didn't understand some patron. Eventually [[Henry Roberts|Henry]] appeared and took [[Robert Smirke|Robert]] away.

[[George Gilbert Scott|George]] started to work with [[William Bonython Moffatt]], a builder's son, on workhouses to [[George Gilbert Scott|George]]'s design. Together they started to poach [[Sampson Kempthorne|Sampson]]'s commissions, something that [[Sampson Kempthorne|Sampson]] found extremely unprofessional. One evening in 1836, around the same time they opened their first workhouse, [[Sampson Kempthorne|Sampson]] was working late. After midnight, although the building was locked he heard steps outside his room along with the tapping of a cane and the jangle of keys. As he heard this, he felt the room pressing in around him so he could hardly breathe or move. After a few minutes the sensation stopped and he heard the footsteps walking away. He ventured out to see who this person was, and saw a short, wide figure wearing a tall black hat that just showed grey wisps of hair. It had a black cane with an iron tip and went into one of the clerk's offices. Upon searching, [[Sampson Kempthorne|Sampson]] found nothing but one of [[George Gilbert Scott|George]]'s plans behind a table.

[[Sampson Kempthorne|Sampson]] went to find [[George Gilbert Scott|George]] on one of his construction sites. He found him talking to a worker that kept asking for 'The Governor', who had had a bunch of keys and who had called his associate, Harry, idle. He was gesturing to a wall which was smooth except for four fingertips sticking out of it.

# Post-Statement

[[Jonathan Sims]] has read up on [[Robert Smirke]], but found nothing about this supposed interest in the esoteric and supernatural. Although [[Henry Roberts]]' own buildings do not appear to have featured hauntings, he worked on Fishmonger's Hall with [[George Gilbert Scott|Scott]], which was supposedly the scene of a few minor hauntings. [[Jonathan Sims|Jonathan]] remarks that [[George Gilbert Scott|Scott]] worked on St. Pancras Station, Albert Memorial, and the restoration of Westminster Abbey, which is concerning if his buildings turn out to be esoteric.
